A Trinet rig is several cameras with no cables between them — head unit and wrist units, each with its own crystal oscillator, each drifting apart by milliseconds every minute if left alone. Multi-view vision needs the opposite: every frame from every camera stamped on one clock, tightly enough that rows of the rolling shutter line up across devices. One sensor row is about 25 µs. That is the bar we measure against.

How it works

Four layers, each measured separately before being trusted together:

1 · Clock transfer. A custom 2.4 GHz protocol runs NTP-style two-way exchanges when idle and passive one-way beacons while recording — every timestamp taken from hardware interrupt edges, never from software, with a self-calibrating per-device path constant and a physical floor filter that rejects impossible measurements.

2 · Shutter phase. Each camera servos its sensor timing onto the group's frame grid, so shutters fire together — and when the radio fades for a beat, the servo coasts through the gap and honestly flags those frames instead of guessing.

3 · Stamping. Every frame is stamped at the temporal centre of its middle row — computed from the sensor's true readout geometry, which the firmware knows from its own registers. No tuning constants, nothing to calibrate per unit.

4 · Honesty. Each frame carries a quality flag and each recording a live error estimate, so downstream software never has to trust — it can check.

Why you can believe these numbers

Everything above is measured outside the system being tested: an LED strobe with randomized timing is filmed by all cameras at once, and its edges — read through the rolling shutter at ~25 µs per row — pin physical simultaneity with no reference to any clock being measured. The method caught every bug the system's own metrics missed.

Spec, stated honestly. Cross-camera timestamp agreement is typically ≤ 50 µs with session-to-session variation of ±42 µs (the radio tracking floor), bounded at ~150 µs worst-case — with per-frame quality flags marking the rare radio-degraded frames (<1%). The stereo pair is sub-microsecond. All figures are optical, out-of-loop measurements on production firmware.
